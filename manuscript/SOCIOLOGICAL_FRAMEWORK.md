# The Institutional Geometry of Codified Repression: A Sociological Framework

*Companion interpretation for the endogenous-enforcement ABM (v2.5–v3.2).*
*Draft — citations are canonical anchors to be verified against `references.bib` before submission.*

## 1. The problem this framework addresses

Repressive orthodoxy in codified moral communities — religious, ideological, or
organizational — is usually explained by one of three agents: extremists who
capture an institution, charismatic leaders who bend it, or external shocks that
harden it. Each explanation locates the cause in a *person* or an *event*. Each
therefore predicts that removing the person, or weathering the event, restores
moderation.

The historical record resists this. The same repressive form recurs across
theologically unrelated traditions, reappears after its leaders are gone, and
returns generations after apparent liberalization. A person-centered account
cannot explain a pattern that outlives every person who instantiates it.

This framework proposes the alternative the model formalizes: **repressive
enforcement is a property of institutional structure, not of belief or
personality.** It is a role the geometry of a codified community creates and
then fills with whoever is available. The contribution of the agent-based model
is to show that this outcome follows *necessarily* from a small set of
structural conditions, using symmetric agents with no pre-assigned roles — so
nothing about the result can be attributed to zealots, leaders, or shocks that
were built in by assumption.

## 2. Central proposition

> Wherever three conditions co-occur — a **legible code** (rules whose violation
> is observable), a **selective reward for enforcing** it, and a **costly or
> sealed exit** — a repressive enforcement apparatus assembles itself from the
> population at hand, concentrates sanctioning in a small cadre, and sustains a
> compliant majority that need not share its beliefs. Remove any one condition
> and the apparatus does not form.

The claim is deliberately conditional. The model does not say codified systems
*always* become repressive; it says repression is a strong attractor *within a
definable region* of institutional parameters, and is absent outside it. This is
what makes the framework falsifiable rather than fatalistic (§7).

## 3. Four microfoundational mechanisms

Each mechanism the model exhibits corresponds to an established sociological
theory. The model's added value is not to originate these ideas but to make them
*compossible in a single generative mechanism* and to rank them by causal weight.

### M1 — Exit closure is the master switch

**Model result.** Sealing the exit is the dominant lever for durable capture:
capture rises from zero to a strong majority as the cost of leaving increases,
while belief intensity, reward magnitude, and leader charisma move it far less.
In the cleanest test (imposed exit-closure, no self-policing channel present),
capture climbs from ~0 to ~89% as the closure parameter rises, with a sharp
threshold near the midpoint.

**Theoretical anchor.** This is Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970)
read as a control system: foreclosing exit forces the remaining options into
loyalty or coerced compliance. It is Goffman's *total institution* (1961) and
Coser's *greedy institution* (1974), whose defining feature is the management of
boundaries rather than of beliefs. It is the economics-of-religion account of
"strict churches" (Iannaccone 1994; Stark and Finke 2000), in which sacrifice,
stigma, and raised exit costs — not doctrine — produce commitment and control.

**Why it matters.** The primacy of exit reframes the causal story. Repression is
not primarily manufactured by ideology; ideology is what a closed boundary makes
enforceable.

### M2 — Compliance without belief

**Model result.** Captured populations are overwhelmingly composed of
non-believers. Genuine adherence remains a small minority (on the order of 10%)
while behavioral compliance approaches unanimity. The apparatus produces
submission, not conversion.

**Theoretical anchor.** This is Kuran's *preference falsification* (1995): under
sanction, private and public preference diverge, and observers cannot read the
first from the second. It is Noelle-Neumann's *spiral of silence* (1974) and
Havel's greengrocer (1978), who displays the slogan he does not believe. Most
directly, it is Centola, Willer, and Macy's *Emperor's Dilemma* (2005), in which
agents enforce a norm they privately reject, producing a self-sustaining
"illusion" of consensus. The present model reproduces that dynamic and embeds it
in a full institutional apparatus with an exit and an enforcer market.

**Why it matters.** It severs repressiveness from conviction. A community's
coercive intensity cannot be inferred from what its members believe, because the
captured and the free hold nearly identical distributions of private conviction.
The diagnostic is the boundary, not the creed.

### M3 — Selective patronage staffs the cadre

**Model result.** A *flat* benefit of membership does not produce capture; an
*enforcement-conditional* reward does. When policing the code confers status,
patronage, and position, a minority accumulates the capital to specialize in
enforcement, and sanctioning concentrates in that minority even though no role
was assigned.

**Theoretical anchor.** This is the logic of *selective incentives* (Olson 1965)
and of the *selectorate* (Bueno de Mesquita et al. 2003): a ruling coalition is
bought with private goods contingent on loyalty, and its members enforce because
their rewards depend on the regime's survival. It is Berman's *club model* of
religious militancy (2009), in which sacrifice screens and selective benefits
bind an enforcing core.

**Why it matters.** The machinery of repression is a career, not a mob. It is
staffed by people for whom loyalty-enforcement pays, which is why it persists
without a supply of true believers and why uniform "everyone benefits" accounts
of cohesion fail to generate it.

### M4 — The enforcer is an emergent role, not a person

**Model result.** With symmetric agents and identical starting conditions but
different random seeds, the *identity* of the enforcing cadre varies while the
*degree* of concentration is nearly invariant (cross-seed variation in the
concentration statistic approaches zero). Different people, same structure.

**Theoretical anchor.** This is multiple realizability applied to social
structure: the position selects the occupant, not the reverse. It resonates with
Merton's *role-set*, with structural accounts in which organizations reproduce
positions independently of who fills them, and with the institutional-isomorphism
tradition (DiMaggio and Powell 1983) in which form recurs across otherwise
unrelated settings.

**Why it matters.** It is the formal ground for the historical observation that
motivates the whole project: the same enforcing role reappears with
interchangeable occupants, because the code manufactures the role and any
adequately positioned agent will fill it.

## 4. Temporal structure: episodic recurrence

**Model result.** When the apparatus can rise and fall, the system does not
settle into permanent capture or permanent freedom. It cycles: a leader emerges
and seizes the apparatus; grievance accumulates during the episode; pushback ends
it (popular revolt, elite coup, or a blend); the apparatus dissolves and the exit
reopens; grievance decays; and because the code itself never changes, a new
leader eventually re-ignites the same latent mechanism. Inter-episode intervals
are irregular. Crucially, the *book is held constant throughout* — recurrence is
driven by the cycling of institutional state, not by any change in the rules.

**Theoretical anchor.** This is the revival-and-reform cycle rendered as a
relaxation oscillator. It connects to Weber's *routinization of charisma* (each
episode re-personalizes and then re-institutionalizes authority), to threshold
and tipping accounts of collective behavior (Granovetter 1978; Schelling 1978),
and to structural-demographic theories of recurrent instability driven by
accumulated grievance and elite competition (Goldstone; Turchin). The two
pushback channels the model exposes map onto two historical signatures:
grievance-driven episodes that recur regularly (popular uprisings) and
coup-driven episodes that punctuate long, entrenched tenures (palace politics).

**Why it matters.** It explains persistence-through-recurrence without appeal to
cultural essence. The canon is a standing affordance; successive movements
exploit it; the pattern belongs to the text's structure, not to any generation's
disposition.

## 5. The transition is a threshold, not a gradient

**Model result.** Outcomes are near-bistable. Communities sit either open-and-
quiet or closed-and-captured, with very few in a stable intermediate state, and
the crossing as exit-closure increases is abrupt rather than smooth.

**Theoretical anchor.** Granovetter's threshold models and Schelling's tipping
dynamics: aggregate behavior flips discontinuously once a critical fraction is
reached. There is no slow slide into repression; there is a boundary, and it is
either open or shut.

**Why it matters.** It accounts for two puzzles at once: why captured systems
look stable and total from inside, and why they can unravel rapidly once the exit
reopens. Both follow from bistability.

## 6. What the model contributes to sociology

1. **Unification.** Four literatures — exit/boundary control, preference
   falsification, selective-incentive coalitions, and threshold dynamics — are
   usually invoked separately. The model shows they are facets of one mechanism
   and specifies how they interlock.
2. **Causal ranking.** It does not merely list conditions; it weighs them. Exit
   closure dominates; selective reward staffs the cadre; self-policing is a
   downstream signature rather than a cause; belief is nearly irrelevant to
   whether capture occurs.
3. **Endogenous recurrence.** It generates the historical wave-pattern from
   within, with a constant code, rather than imposing exogenous shocks to explain
   episodes.
4. **A microfoundation without built-in villains.** Because agents are symmetric
   and unassigned, the repressive structure cannot be an artifact of assumed
   zealotry. That is the strongest form of the claim.

## 7. Falsifiable implications (why this should convince a skeptic)

A framework persuades not by fitting the past but by risking a prediction its
rivals do not share. This one makes several.

- **Measure boundaries, not creeds.** The framework predicts that a community's
  coercive intensity is forecast by its *exit cost* (economic dependence,
  shunning severity, informational closure, physical isolation) and *not* by
  survey measures of member belief. A belief-centered account predicts the
  opposite. The two are empirically separable.
- **Compliance–belief gap.** It predicts a large and *stable* gap between public
  compliance and private conviction in captured communities, detectable by
  methods that recover private preference (list experiments, endorsement
  experiments, behavior after exit becomes cheap).
- **Reopening test.** It predicts rapid de-repression when exit costs fall, even
  with doctrine and leadership unchanged — because capture was never held up by
  belief.
- **Cadre composition.** It predicts enforcers are selected by
  position-and-payoff, not by measured zeal; enforcer status should track
  accumulated institutional capital more than devotion.
- **Recurrence tied to canon persistence.** It predicts that traditions whose
  codes remain legible and enforceable will show *recurrent* episodes with
  interchangeable leaders, whereas traditions that lose code-legibility (through
  reinterpretation, pluralization, or loss of enforcement affordance) will not
  reignite even when charismatic figures appear.

Each prediction is one a person- or belief-centered theory would get wrong.

## 8. Scope conditions and honest limits

The model is a formal argument, not a measurement of any particular community.
Its results establish that these outcomes *follow* from the structure; they do
not establish that any specific historical case was inevitable, and the
parameter values are illustrative rather than estimated. The framework's
empirical purchase rests on the testable implications in §7, which locate the
theory's risk in the world rather than in the simulation. The claim is
structural and conditional throughout: not that codified moral systems must
become repressive, but that they will *wherever the geometry closes* — and that
where it does not, they remain quiet regardless of doctrine or leadership.
